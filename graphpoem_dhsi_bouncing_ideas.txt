Absolutely — and my first instinct is that this is **more feasible on an M2 Mac than it may initially appear**, provided you resist treating the four years of material as one giant multimodal deep-learning problem.

The crucial move is to make the retrospective a **retrieval-and-relation problem**:

> **poem/corpus → feature trajectory → candidate livestream moments → relations of consonance/contrast → montage**

rather than:

> **all poems + all video + all audio → train a huge model → hope meaning emerges**.

That also seems philosophically much closer to the project you describe: the inverse mapping need not recover *the* poem behind a livestream segment. It can recover a **set of poems, corpus regions, and relational neighbourhoods** to which that segment belongs—or against which it becomes legible.

Having looked through the repositories, I think the project has an unusually strong **technical and conceptual continuity already built into it**. The new work does not need a new grand model. It can be the next operation in the same chain:

**poem → features → sound/video relations → temporal/interplatform dynamics → audiovisual composition**

and now:

**corpus ↔ livestream archive → relational retrieval → retrospective montage.**

Your existing work already explicitly maps, for example, syllable density to frame density, tempo to motion intensity, pacing variance to transition sharpness, fricative density to edge dynamics, plosive density to scene-change frequency, vocal smoothness to luminance variance, and silence to visual stillness.  The DHSI work, meanwhile, already gives you a second layer: the community as a hybrid interplatform dynamical system whose evolution can become non-linearly ruptured or evental. ([GitHub][1]) And the creative Singularity project already formulates the move from text/graph structure into motion, sound, and cinematic sequence as *panphrastic transmediation*. ([GitHub][2])

## 1. I would not think of the inverse as a single inverse function

I think the most productive object here is a **relation-valued inverse**.

Your forward pipeline is roughly:

[
P \xrightarrow{F} X_P \xrightarrow{M} X_{AV} \xrightarrow{R} AV
]

where a poem (P) produces a feature representation (X_P), which is mapped into audiovisual features and then rendered.

But the inverse problem is underdetermined:

[
AV \nrightarrow P
]

There is no reason one livestream fragment should return one poem.

Instead:

[
AV_t \xrightarrow{F^{-1}*{rel}} {(P_i,w_i,r_i)}*{i=1}^{k}
]

where each video/audio window (AV_t) retrieves a **set of poems**, with:

* (w_i): strength of relation;
* (r_i): type of relation.

For example:

* **consonance** — close feature behaviour;
* **contrast** — systematically opposite behaviour;
* **resonance** — similar temporal shape despite different absolute values;
* **anticipation** — a livestream moment resembles a later corpus state;
* **afterimage** — a moment retains the signature of an earlier corpus state;
* **evental rupture** — correspondence breaks down.

That last category feels particularly important. The inverse should not only ask:

> *What poem does this moment look like?*

It should also ask:

> *Where does the livestream refuse poetic retrieval?*

That failure can itself become material.

## 2. The basic computational unit should be a window, not a whole poem or whole livestream

I would make a common unit:

[
W = \text{a temporal window}
]

Perhaps initially 5–15 seconds for livestream material, with overlap.

For each window, calculate only the features that your existing system can already meaningfully compare.

Something like:

```text
year
source_type          poem | livestream | social | jupyter | event
source_id
t_start
t_end

syllable/frame_density
tempo/motion
pacing/transition
fricative/edge
plosive/scene_change
smoothness/luminance
silence/stillness

affect_1 ... affect_n
temporal_descriptors
embedding coordinates
```

The important thing is that you **do not have to decode every possible property of four years of video**. You build a relatively small common feature table.

Then the retrospective becomes a nearest-neighbour problem.

For each corpus region (C_j):

[
N^+(C_j)=\operatorname{arg,min}_k d(C_j,L_t)
]

for consonant livestream windows, and perhaps:

[
N^-(C_j)=\operatorname{arg,max}_k d(C_j,L_t)
]

for deliberately contrastive ones.

But I would use a weighted distance rather than one total Euclidean distance:

[
d_w(x,y)=
\sum_m \alpha_m d_m(x_m,y_m)
]

The weights (\alpha_m) could change by year—or, even better, become an artistic parameter.

So one montage pass might privilege:

* phonological/sonic relations;

another:

* affective relations;

another:

* temporal flow;

another:

* graph/community relations.

This gives you **several inverse maps of the same archive**, rather than pretending there is one correct inverse.

## 3. A particularly exciting possibility: use trajectory matching, not just feature matching

This may be where the piece becomes genuinely retrospective rather than merely archival.

Instead of asking whether a 10-second livestream segment resembles a poem, ask whether its **change through time** resembles the development of a poem/corpus segment.

For a feature (f):

[
\Delta f(t)=f(t+1)-f(t)
]

Then compare trajectories:

[
\mathbf{x}*{t:t+n}
\sim
\mathbf{p}*{s:s+n}
]

So:

* increasing tempo ↔ increasing motion;
* sudden phonological compression ↔ visual densification;
* affective oscillation ↔ alternating visual/sonic intensity;
* silence ↔ stabilization;
* rupture ↔ graph disturbance.

This seems especially compatible with your *Affect and Temporal Flows* work, and with the DHSI model's concern with continuous evolution interrupted by spikes or singularities. ([GitHub][1])

I would therefore build two retrieval modes:

### A. State retrieval

> Find me the livestream moments most like this poem/corpus state.

### B. Trajectory retrieval

> Find me livestream moments that undergo the most similar transformation.

The second one could be far more narratively powerful.

## 4. The two journeys can use the *same machinery* but different starting conditions

This, to me, is the conceptual key.

### Journey I: MARGENTO

The place poem is not merely an opening text.

It becomes an **initial condition**.

[
x_0^{M} = F(\text{place poem})
]

You then let the archive answer:

> Across four years, what moments are retrieved by—or become increasingly distant from—this initial condition?

The journey can therefore move through:

1. **origin** — the place poem;
2. **first correspondences** — archive moments closest to it;
3. **deformation** — moments that retain some features but transform others;
4. **dispersion** — the place signature distributed across the community;
5. **return** — a later moment that unexpectedly retrieves the original state;
6. **non-return** — a final relation where no satisfactory inverse exists.

That gives you a personal journey without requiring voice-over biography or conventional chronology.

### Journey II: #GraphPoem

Here the initial condition is not a place poem but a **community configuration**.

Your DHSI model is already explicitly concerned with the interplatform system comprising participants, coding, AI/ML agents, poetry data, and multiple platform logs. ([GitHub][1])

So:

[
G(t_0) \rightarrow G(t_1) \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow G(t_e)
]

and at some (t_e):

[
\left|\text{prediction}(G(t_e))-G(t_e)\right|
\gg 0
]

The montage need not simply illustrate that singularity.

It can use the four-year archive to ask:

> **What were its precursors?**

and perhaps:

> **Did the singularity leave traces that the earlier mapping system can retrieve retrospectively?**

That could produce an extraordinarily nice temporal fold:

**the event is detected in 2023, but its conditions are searched for backward and forward through the archive.**

## 5. I would explicitly make a third structure: the braid

Rather than alternating "personal chapter / community chapter", I would represent both as trajectories in the same relational space.

For each temporal point:

[
M(t), \quad G(t)
]

and compute:

[
d(M(t),G(t))
]

The montage can then organize itself around:

### Convergence

Personal and communal trajectories approach one another.

### Parallelism

They move similarly without touching.

### Interference

One becomes the inverse or contrast of the other.

### Capture

A community moment retrieves the place poem better than a personal archival moment does.

### Escape

The community enters a configuration for which the personal mapping no longer has an equivalent.

### Singularity

Both mappings become unstable.

That would make the two journeys genuinely intertwined rather than two stories edited together.

## 6. On the M2: I would build a deliberately "small-data" pipeline

I would strongly recommend **not processing the original videos repeatedly**.

### Stage 1 — proxy generation

Use FFmpeg to make low-resolution proxies:

* perhaps 480p;
* perhaps 8–12 fps for feature extraction.

Keep originals untouched for final editing.

### Stage 2 — sparse sampling

Do not run expensive analysis on every frame.

For your current features, many can be estimated from:

* every nth frame;
* scene-change candidates;
* fixed temporal windows.

You already have a mapping that uses relatively classical CV operations such as frame differences, histogram relations, edge behaviour, and luminance variance. That is very good news for an M2: this is considerably lighter than trying to run a giant vision model over years of footage. 

### Stage 3 — cache everything

The most important practical rule:

> **Video is raw material. Feature tables are the working medium.**

Once a livestream has become:

```text
livestream_windows.parquet
```

with perhaps 10,000–100,000 rows depending on segmentation, you should be able to explore relations without reopening the video.

Store:

```text
features/
    2022_livestream_windows.parquet
    2023_livestream_windows.parquet
    2024_livestream_windows.parquet
    2025_livestream_windows.parquet

indexes/
    consonance_index.npy
    contrast_index.npy
    trajectory_index.npy
```

Then only decode the original high-resolution video for the clips you actually select.

## 7. I would begin with one year only

This is probably my strongest practical recommendation.

Don't start with:

> four years × all poems × all video × all platforms.

Start with:

> **one year × 10 significant corpus regions × one livestream.**

Build the entire pipeline on that.

Your first output should be something wonderfully simple, like:

| Corpus region | Top consonant clip | Top contrastive clip | Relation score |
| ------------- | ------------------ | -------------------- | -------------- |
| C1            | 00:14:32–00:14:47  | 01:06:10–01:06:25    | ...            |
| C2            | ...                | ...                  | ...            |

Then watch it.

This human viewing stage is crucial. The model should propose, but the montage should not be mechanically generated.

I would think of the system as:

[
\text{archive}
\rightarrow
\text{feature extraction}
\rightarrow
\text{candidate retrieval}
\rightarrow
\boxed{\text{your viewing}}
\rightarrow
\text{selection}
\rightarrow
\text{montage}
]

That boxed stage is not a weakness in the computational method. It is precisely where your analytical-creative methodology remains performative.

## 9. One thing I would add that may be especially fruitful: an uncertainty score

For every inverse retrieval, calculate not only the best match but the **ambiguity of the match**.

For instance, if the nearest 20 clips are all similarly close:

[
U(x)=\text{high}
]

the poem disperses across the archive.

If one clip is dramatically closer:

[
U(x)=\text{low}
]

the relation is concentrated.

That distinction itself could become montage structure:

* **high certainty** → long sustained shot;
* **ambiguity** → rapid superposition of many candidate clips;
* **contrast** → hard cut;
* **singularity** → retrieval failure, noise, blackout, or a return to raw material.

In other words, you could sonify/visualize not only the poem-to-video relation, but the **confidence topology of the inverse relation**.

That feels beautifully aligned with your existing concern with what can and cannot be learned. The DHSI project explicitly frames a situation in which the model tracks relatively near-linear evolution but encounters sharply increased error around rupture. ([GitHub][1])

## 10. My strongest overall suggestion

I would formulate the work computationally as a **two-seed, many-path retrospective graph**.

The nodes would be:

* poems;
* corpus segments;
* livestream windows;
* performances;
* social interactions;
* coding/data events;
* singularity/event windows.

The edges would be typed:

```text
CONSONANT
CONTRASTIVE
TRAJECTORIALLY_SIMILAR
TEMPORALLY_PRECEDES
TEMPORALLY_FOLLOWS
ANTICIPATES
RETRIEVES
FAILS_TO_RETRIEVE
EVENTALLY_RUPTURES
```

Then you have two privileged starting nodes:

[
S_M = \text{the place poem}
]

and:

[
S_G = \text{the community/event origin}
]

The retrospective montage is then not a summary of four years.

It is a **traversal of that graph**.

Sometimes the two traversals share nodes.

Sometimes they cross.

Sometimes one path predicts or retrieves the other.

And perhaps the climax is precisely where graph traversal itself becomes unstable—where the singularity is not merely represented as an event in the footage, but experienced as a breakdown or multiplication of the possible relations.

That seems to me the genuinely exciting continuation of the work you already have: not simply using computational analysis to *illustrate an archive*, but allowing the archive to **answer back through the inverse, one-to-many mappings**.

The most immediate, M2-friendly prototype I would build is therefore:

**one corpus year → 10 corpus windows → 1 livestream → 10 consonant + 10 contrastive + 10 trajectory matches → contact sheets → manual selection of a 3–5 minute micro-montage.**

If that works aesthetically, the four-year version becomes scaling, caching, and refinement—not a leap into a new technical universe.
